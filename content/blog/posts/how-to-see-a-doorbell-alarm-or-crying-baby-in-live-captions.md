If you cannot hear a doorbell, a timer or a baby, you have three kinds of tools: an app that writes the sound into your captions, a phone setting that sends an alert, and a device made for the sound (a doorbell light, a bed shaker). Captio AI, a live captioning app for deaf and hard of hearing people, added the first kind on iPhone in version 1.1.5 [1]. This guide shows what each tool does, where each one stops, and which job needs a dedicated device.

## What does Captio AI show besides speech?

Captio AI 1.1.5 shows a doorbell, an alarm, a crying baby, a barking dog, laughter or music in your captions, written like [doorbell rings] [1]. Alert sounds make your iPhone vibrate [1]. You change it in Settings under Recognize sounds [1].

That is everything the release notes confirm. They do not give a full list of sounds, and they do not say the app listens when captions are off. Plan around what is stated: sounds appear in captions you are already reading.

## How do Apple and Android sound alerts compare?

Apple's Sound Recognition and Android's Sound Notifications alert you to sounds around you without captions, while Captio AI puts sounds inside the captions of a conversation. The table compares what each company states on its own page.

| | Captio AI (iPhone, 1.1.5) | Apple Sound Recognition (iPhone) | Android Sound Notifications |
|---|---|---|---|
| Where you see it | In the captions, like [doorbell rings] [1] | A notification [2] | Vibrate or camera flash [3] |
| Sounds named | Doorbell, alarm, crying baby, barking dog, laughter, music [1] | "15 different sounds", including sirens, horns and crying babies [2] | Smoke and fire alarms, sirens, baby sounds, dog barking, knocking, doorbell, appliance beeping, landline ringing, water running [3] |
| Alert | Phone vibrates for alerts [1] | Notification [2] | Vibrate or flash [3] |
| Custom sounds | Not stated | Yes, sounds unique to your environment [2] | Not stated |
| Where it runs | iPhone [1] | iPhone and other Apple devices [2] | Pixel and some phones; others via an app [3] |

Apple and Google are better when you want an alert with no captions running: Apple's page says Sound Recognition "listens for certain sounds" and uses on-device intelligence to notify you [2], and Google says Sound Notifications are always listening, which can use more battery [3]. Captio AI puts the sound in the same place as the words, which suits you when you are already following a conversation.

## How do I use sound captions at home, at dinner or in a lecture?

Captio AI's sound captions fit three everyday situations, and each one uses the same step: start Live Captions and keep the phone where it hears the room.

- **At home, everyday conversations:** a doorbell or an alarm shows next to what your family says, so you know why everyone stopped talking. See [everyday conversations](/use-cases/everyday-conversations).
- **At a family dinner or a group setting:** laughter and music show in the captions, so you know a joke landed or that music is why the table is hard to follow. See [group settings](/use-cases/group-settings).
- **In a lecture or a meeting:** laughter shows in the captions as a sound, so you know the room laughed even when the words before it were hard to follow. See [lectures](/use-cases/lectures) and [meetings](/use-cases/meetings).
- **In a restaurant:** when music plays, it can show as a sound in the captions next to the words you can follow, which explains why the table is hard to hear. See [restaurants](/use-cases/restaurants).

Start with [Live Captions](/live-captions) and check Settings, Recognize sounds. Google advises placing the phone in the middle of the room, unobstructed, for its own alerts [3]; the same sense applies to any phone that has to hear the room.

## Can a phone alert replace a smoke alarm for a deaf person?

No. A phone alert cannot replace a smoke alarm made for deaf and hard of hearing people, and Apple says not to rely on Sound Recognition in circumstances where you may be harmed or injured, or in high-risk or emergency situations [2].

The reason comes from research. A 2007 study by Dorothy Bruck and Ian Thomas of Victoria University in Australia found that the standard 3100 Hz smoke alarm tone failed to wake 43% of people with mild to moderately severe hearing loss, even though they could hear it while awake [4]. Strobe lights woke only 27% of the hard of hearing participants, while a 520 Hz square wave woke 92% at 75 dBA and 100% at 95 dBA [4]. For deaf and hard of hearing sleepers, that points to a loud low-frequency alarm or a bed shaker connected to the detector, which your local fire service can advise on. This is information, not safety advice for your home.

## Which tool should you use?

Use the tool that matches the moment, and keep a made-for-purpose device for anything that could hurt you.

| Situation | Better choice |
|---|---|
| Following a conversation at home and you want the doorbell in the captions | Captio AI sound captions [1] |
| An alert with no captions running, on an iPhone | Apple Sound Recognition [2] |
| An alert with vibration or flash on Android | Android Sound Notifications [3] |
| Fire or smoke while you sleep | A smoke alarm with a low-frequency signal or bed shaker, not a phone app [2][4] |

Captio AI is for in-person speech and the sounds around it. It does not caption phone calls.

## What is not confirmed yet?

Captio AI's release notes name six sounds and vibration for alerts, nothing more [1]. Android availability of sound captions is not confirmed, so this guide says iPhone only. Accuracy figures are not published, so test the doorbell, the alarm and the baby sound yourself, in your own home, before you depend on them.
