Hearing loss is one of the most common health conditions in the world, and one of the least visible. The most complete estimate, from the Global Burden of Disease study published in The Lancet, puts the number at 1.57 billion people in 2019, or one person in five [1]. Most of them can still hear some speech: about three in four, 1.17 billion people, have mild hearing loss [1]. A smaller group, more than 70 million people, are deaf [3].

This page collects the main figures in one place, from the global picture down to the US and the UK, with each number linked to where it comes from.

## The numbers at a glance

| Measure | Number | Source |

| Moderate or worse hearing loss, after accounting for hearing aid use | 403 million | The Lancet [1] |
| People who need care for disabling hearing loss | 430 million, over 5% of the world | WHO [2] |
| Children with disabling hearing loss | 34 million | WHO [2] |
| Children aged 5 to 19 with any hearing loss | 95.1 million | WHO [2] |
| Deaf people | more than 70 million | World Federation of the Deaf [3] |
| Sign languages in use | more than 300 | World Federation of the Deaf [3] |
| People with hearing loss by 2050 | nearly 2.5 billion, one in four | WHO [2][6] |
| Yearly cost of unaddressed hearing loss | almost US$1 trillion | WHO [2] |

The two headline numbers measure different things. The 1.57 billion counts everyone with any hearing loss, including mild loss that someone may not notice day to day. The 430 million counts people whose hearing loss is disabling, which the WHO defines as a loss greater than 35 decibels in the better ear [2].

The WHO's 430 million comes from the same Global Burden of Disease estimate: 430.4 million people had moderate or worse hearing loss in the better ear in 2019, and 403.3 million still did after accounting for the hearing aids some of them use [1].

## What do deaf, hard of hearing and disabling hearing loss mean?

The WHO uses **hard of hearing** for people with hearing loss that ranges from mild to severe. People who are hard of hearing usually communicate in spoken language and can benefit from hearing aids, cochlear implants, other assistive devices and captioning [2].

**Deaf** people mostly have profound hearing loss, which means very little or no hearing [2]. For many deaf people, sign language is an important way to communicate [6]. Worldwide, deaf communities use more than 300 different sign languages [3].

**Disabling hearing loss** is the WHO's threshold for when hearing loss needs care: more than 35 dB in the better hearing ear [2].

## Who has hearing loss?

Hearing loss becomes more common with age [2]. Of all people with a hearing impairment, 62.1% are older than 50 [1]. Among people older than 60, more than a quarter have disabling hearing loss [2].

National figures show the same pattern:

- In the UK, more than half of people aged 55 or over have hearing loss, and almost 80% of people over 70 will have it [5].
- In the US, age is the strongest predictor of hearing loss among adults aged 20 to 69, with the most hearing loss in the 60 to 69 age group [4].

Hearing loss also starts young for many people. About 95.1 million children and teenagers aged 5 to 19 live with hearing loss [2], and in the US about 2 to 3 of every 1,000 children are born with a detectable hearing loss in one or both ears [4].

## Where do people with hearing loss live?

Nearly 80% of people with disabling hearing loss live in low- and middle-income countries [2]. The largest number of people with moderate to complete hearing loss live in the WHO's Western Pacific region, which includes China, Japan, South Korea and Australia: 127.1 million people [1].

Low-income countries are also short of specialists. Among low-income countries, about 78% have fewer than one ear, nose and throat specialist per million people, and 93% have fewer than one audiologist per million [6].

## How many people in the US and UK have hearing loss?

In the US, about 37.5 million adults reported some trouble hearing in the 2012 National Health Interview Survey [4]. In the UK, more than 18 million people are deaf, have hearing loss or have tinnitus [5].

| Country | Figure | Source |
|---|---|---|
| United States | About 15% of adults, 37.5 million people, reported some trouble hearing in the 2012 National Health Interview Survey | NIDCD [4] |
| United States | 2 to 3 of every 1,000 children are born with detectable hearing loss | NIDCD [4] |
| United States | Fewer than 1 in 3 adults over 70 who could benefit from hearing aids have ever used them | NIDCD [4] |
| United Kingdom | More than 18 million people, 1 in 3, are deaf, have hearing loss or tinnitus | RNID [5] |
| United Kingdom | 2.4 million would struggle to hear most conversational speech | RNID [5] |

The UK figure counts people who are deaf, people with hearing loss and people with tinnitus together, and it counts hearing levels of 25 dB or worse in one or both ears [5]. The global one in five counts only hearing loss of more than 20 dB in the better ear [1], so the two figures are measured differently and shouldn't be compared directly.

## Will the number of people with hearing loss keep growing?

The number of people with hearing loss will keep growing. The Lancet study projects 2.45 billion people with hearing loss by 2050, a 56.1% increase from 2019 [1]. The rate of hearing loss at any given age is expected to stay about the same. The number grows because the world's population is growing and getting older [1].

The WHO expects more than 700 million people to need hearing care by 2050 [2]. It also warns that over 1 billion young adults are at risk of permanent, avoidable hearing loss from unsafe listening practices [2].

Care is affordable at scale. The WHO estimates that less than US$1.40 extra per person per year would expand ear and hearing care worldwide, with a return of nearly US$16 for every dollar invested over ten years [2].

## What these numbers mean in daily life

Most of the 1.57 billion people with hearing loss are not deaf. They hear some speech, rely on spoken language, and lose words when people talk fast, turn away, speak over each other or sit in a noisy room. For them, the WHO lists captioning alongside hearing aids and cochlear implants as a way to follow spoken language [2], and its 2021 report names captioning and sign language interpretation as services that improve access to communication and education [6].
