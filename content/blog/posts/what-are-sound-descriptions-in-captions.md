Sound descriptions are the short notes inside captions that tell you what you cannot hear: a door slams, music plays, an audience laughs. Captions that include them are often called SDH, subtitles for the deaf and hard of hearing. This explainer covers the rules that TV and streaming captioners follow, how they differ, and which live captioning tools write sounds into the text of a conversation today.

## What is a sound description in captions?

A sound description is a note in the caption text, usually in brackets, that names a sound other than speech, such as [door slams] or [applause]. The Captioning Key from the Described and Captioned Media Program (DCMP) defines sound effects as "sounds other than music, narration, or dialogue" [1]. It says they are captioned "if it is necessary for the understanding and/or enjoyment of the media" [1].

Without them, a caption file only carries the words. A hearing viewer also hears the phone ring before a character answers it. A sound description gives that cue to a deaf viewer in the same moment.

## What is the difference between SDH, closed captions and subtitles?

SDH subtitles are subtitles with extra information for deaf and hard of hearing viewers, while plain subtitles cover only spoken words. The captioning company Verbit says subtitles "are designed for hearing users", and that SDH adds "speaker tags, sound effects and other elements outside of speech" [9]. It describes closed captions as typically white text on a black background, which sets them apart from SDH [9].

The words vary by country, and this is a vendor's explanation, not a standard. In the US, TV rules use the term closed captioning [3]. The sound rules in the next sections apply whatever you call the file.

## What do US rules require for sound and music in captions?

US television captions must include music, sound effects and audience reaction to the greatest extent possible. The FCC rule at 47 CFR 79.1 says captioning "shall provide nonverbal information that is not observable, such as the identity of speakers", and covers "the existence of music" (with or without lyrics), sound effects and audience reaction [3].

The same rule sets four quality standards: captions must be accurate, synchronous, complete and appropriately placed [3]. Synchronous means captions "shall begin to appear at the time that the corresponding speech or sounds begin" [3]. A [door slams] that appears two seconds late is a quality problem, not only a style problem.

This is a rule for television programming in the US. It does not tell a theatre, a lecture hall or a doctor's office what to do. For those, the Americans with Disabilities Act (ADA) asks for "effective communication", and ADA.gov lists open captioning, closed captioning and real-time captioning (CART) among the aids and services [8].

## How do the DCMP and Netflix rules compare?

The DCMP Captioning Key and Netflix's English style guide agree on brackets and lower case, and disagree on italics. The table sets the two side by side for the points that change what you see on screen.

| Question | DCMP Captioning Key | Netflix English style guide |
|---|---|---|
| When is a sound captioned? | When necessary for understanding or enjoyment; background sounds only when essential to the plot [1] | Plot-pertinent sound effects always, unless the visuals make them clear; only when the sound cannot be visually identified [2] |
| Format | In brackets, including the source of the sound [1] | Brackets around speaker IDs or sound effects [2] |
| Case | Lower case [1] | All lower case except proper nouns [2] |
| Source of the sound | May be left out if clearly visible on screen [1] | Describe the sound, not the visual action [2] |
| Italics | Off-screen sound effects italicised, if italics are available [1] | Never italicise sound effects, even in a voice-over [2] |
| Background music | Bracketed description when essential; music shorter than 5 seconds is not captioned [1] | Not covered in the sections reviewed [2] |
| Song lyrics | Caption lyrics verbatim [1] | Enclosed in a music note (♪) at the start and end of each subtitle [2] |
| Tense | Present tense only [1] | No tense rule found in the sections reviewed [2] |

Two rules from the DCMP matter for readers. Use specific words rather than vague ones, and avoid subjective words such as "beautiful" or "melodic" when describing music, so the note tells you the mood without giving an opinion [1]. And caption background sound "only when they are essential to the plot" [1].

## Why does the verb tense of a sound description matter?

Sound descriptions use the present tense because captions run in step with the sound. The Captioning Key says: "Never use the past tense when describing sounds" [1]. It asks for the present participle for a sustained sound (a phone ringing) and the third person verb for an abrupt one (a door slams) [1].

For a deaf viewer this is practical. [phone rings] tells you the sound is happening now and you may want to look up. [phone rang] would suggest you already missed it.

## Do live captioning tools describe sounds?

Some do, some do not, and the settings differ. The table lists what each tool's own page says. Sound labels in live tools are an extra layer on top of speech, and none of the pages read promises that every sound is caught.

| | Captio AI (iPhone, 1.1.5) | Google Live Caption (Android) | Google Live Transcribe (Android) | Apple Live Captions (iPhone) |
|---|---|---|---|---|

| How to switch it | Settings, Recognize sounds [7] | The "Show sound labels" setting [4] | Settings, Additional settings, Show sound labels [5] | Not applicable |
| Alert for sounds | The phone vibrates for alerts [7] | Not stated | Not stated | Separate Sound Recognition notifies you for 15 sounds [6] |

Captio AI, a live captioning app for deaf and hard of hearing people, writes the sound inside the captions of a conversation. Its release notes show the bracket style the TV rules use, [doorbell rings], in the present tense [7]. Google's pages are the better choice when you use Android, and Apple's Sound Recognition is the better choice when you want an alert with no captions running [4][5][6]. The release notes do not give a full list of sounds or accuracy figures for Captio AI, so test the sounds that matter to you.

## Where do sound descriptions help in TV, lectures and everyday life?

Sound descriptions matter most where a sound changes what the words mean. These are the settings the sources support:

- **TV and media in the US:** the FCC rule requires music, sound effects and audience reaction in captions to the greatest extent possible [3]. See [TV and media](/use-cases/tv-media).
- **Streaming in English:** Netflix's style guide asks for plot-pertinent sound effects, unless the picture makes them clear [2].
- **Lectures and conferences:** CART is one of the aids ADA.gov names for effective communication [8]. The sources read do not say whether CART captioners write laughter or applause. See [lectures](/use-cases/lectures) and [conferences](/use-cases/conferences).
- **Everyday conversations at home:** Captio AI 1.1.5 shows a doorbell or an alarm in live captions next to the words [7]. See [everyday conversations](/use-cases/everyday-conversations).
- **Restaurants and group settings:** Captio AI 1.1.5 lists laughter and music among the sounds it writes into live captions [7]. See [restaurants](/use-cases/restaurants) and [group settings](/use-cases/group-settings).

## What are the limits of sound descriptions?

A sound description is a short note chosen by a person or a program, so it can miss a sound or name the wrong one. TV rules ask for sounds "to the greatest extent possible" and the DCMP asks for judgement about which sounds matter [1][3]. Live tools add a second limit: they decide in real time, with no editor.

A caption is also not a safety alarm. For a smoke alarm or a fire, use a device made for deaf and hard of hearing people.
