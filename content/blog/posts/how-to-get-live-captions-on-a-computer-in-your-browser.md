A Windows 11 laptop, a Mac with Apple silicon or a desktop computer can caption a meeting or a lecture without any extra software, if you point it at the microphone. This guide covers the setup for each, why a browser's captions usually don't work for a room, and where Captio AI, a live captioning app for deaf and hard of hearing people, now fits in. All details come from the makers' own pages, opened in November 2026.

## At a glance: which computer option captions the room?

Windows 11 and Macs with Apple silicon can caption speech picked up by the microphone, while Chrome's Live Caption is described for audio media. The table shows what each page states.

| Option | Captions speech in the room? | Requirement | Where the audio is processed |
|---|---|---|---|
| Windows 11 Live captions | Yes, after you turn on microphone audio [1] | Windows 11, version 22H2 or later [1] | On the device [1] |
| Mac Live Captions | Yes, with the Microphone setting [2] | Mac with Apple silicon [2] | On the device [2] |
| Chrome Live Caption | Not stated; page describes audio media [3] | Chrome on Windows, Mac or Linux [3] | Locally, never stored [3] |
| Captio AI on the web | Not stated for the web version [4] | Any computer, same account as the app [4] | Not stated for the web version |

## How do you turn on live captions in Windows 11 for a meeting?

You turn on Live captions in Windows 11 under Settings, Accessibility, Captions, and then switch on microphone audio so the room is captioned [1]. Microsoft also lists the quick settings and the keyboard shortcut Windows key plus Ctrl plus L [1].

The microphone step matters for a deaf or hard of hearing person in a meeting room. Microsoft says the Include microphone audio option, found in Preferences, is off by default when you turn on live captions [1]. Until you switch it on, the captions follow the sound your computer plays, such as a video call, and a colleague talking next to you stays silent on screen. Two more details from Microsoft's page matter in a room. Live captions pays attention to the default sound output device set in Settings, System, Sound, and Microsoft says you might need to change the default device for audio to be picked up [1]. And "Sound audio will be prioritized over microphone audio" [1]: Microsoft's example is a virtual meeting where a remote participant and you speak over each other, and the captions then show the remote participant, not you [1]. My reading, not Microsoft's: in a hybrid meeting, people in the room could drop out of the captions while someone remote is talking, so check it in a test call. Place the laptop between you and the speakers, and consider a phone captioning app as a second view.

Microsoft lists speech recognition for Chinese, Danish, English in several regional variants, French, German, Italian, Japanese, Korean, Portuguese and Spanish, among others [1]. A [German meeting](/use-cases/meetings/german) or a Spanish seminar is therefore covered; check the full list for your language.

## How do you turn on live captions on a Mac for a lecture?

On a Mac you open System Settings, choose Accessibility, click Live Captions and turn it on, then click the Microphone icon in the Live Captions window to switch between the Mac's audio output and the microphone [2]. Apple describes the microphone as picking up sound around the computer, such as someone speaking in the room [2]. Apple states that Live Captions is available only on Mac computers with Apple silicon [2].

For a [lecture](/use-cases/lectures) the Microphone choice is the one to remember, because Apple says that by default Live Captions transcribes audio from all apps [2]. Apple says the feature is not available in all languages, countries or regions, and points to its feature availability page [2]. Audio is processed on the device [2].

## Does Chrome's Live Caption caption a lecture hall?

Chrome's Live Caption is described by Google as captioning "videos, podcasts, games, live streams, video calls, or other audio media" in Chrome [3]. The page does not mention a microphone, so it should not be your plan for a classroom or a conference room.

Chrome does have one useful trait for lectures that are recorded: Google says audio and captions are processed locally, never stored, and never leave your device [3]. To turn it on, open Chrome, go to Settings, then Accessibility, and turn on Live Caption [3]. Other languages download as language packs [3]. If your lecture is a recorded video that plays in Chrome, this is the quickest option. For a live room, use the system feature or an app.

## What does Captio AI on the web add?

Captio AI on the web is a way to open Captio AI on a computer: the App Store notes for version 1.1.7 say to open captioai.app/app on any computer and sign in with the same account [4]. In the iPhone app you find it in Settings [4].

That is all the notes confirm. They do not say which features the web version has, which browsers it supports or whether it captions through the computer's microphone, so this guide does not promise any of that. If you plan to use it for a [meeting](/use-cases/meetings) or a lecture, open captioai.app/app the day before, sign in and test it with a colleague talking across the table.

What the app itself does is documented on the [Live Captions](/live-captions) page: speech in the room appears word by word on your phone. A phone beside the laptop is often a good setup anyway, since it sits closer to the speaker than a laptop screen does.

## Which setup fits which situation?

Each situation below names the setup that fits it best, based on what the pages state.

- **A team meeting in a room, on Windows 11:** Windows Live captions with microphone audio on, laptop in the middle of the table [1].
- **A lecture, on a Mac with Apple silicon:** Mac Live Captions with Microphone selected, Mac close to the lecturer [2].
- **A recorded lecture playing in Chrome:** Chrome's Live Caption, which Google describes for audio media [3].
- **A doctor's appointment or job interview in person:** a phone on the table, since a laptop is awkward; see the [doctor's appointment page](/use-cases/doctor-appointments) for how Captio AI is used there.
- **A group at a dinner or in a noisy room:** a phone app that labels speakers, covered in [how to see who is talking in live captions](/blog/how-to-see-who-is-talking-in-live-captions).

Whichever you pick, test it before the real conversation, and ask the organiser about a human captioner where you are entitled to one. Apple itself says Live Captions should not be relied upon in high-risk or emergency situations [2]. If a microphone is the weak point, [choosing the right microphone](/blog/how-to-choose-the-microphone-for-live-captions-on-iphone) is worth a read.

## Are there browser tools for live captions?

Web Captioner, a speech-to-text tool, is no longer offered: its page says "Web Captioner has sunset", and its author says they open sourced it in 2023 [5]. If you find a browser captioning tool, check whether it is still maintained and what it says about where audio goes. Chrome's Live Caption has a current Google help page, and Google describes it for media [3]. For a room, the system features above or a phone app are the options with a maker's own help page.

## Where the built-in options are the better choice

Windows 11 Live captions and Mac Live Captions are the better choice when you want captions of what the computer plays together with speech around it, with no sign-up and no app to install. Both process audio on the device, per Microsoft and Apple [1][2], so nothing leaves the machine. Captio AI needs an internet connection for live captions, and its free plan is 100 free credits a day, as of October 2026.
