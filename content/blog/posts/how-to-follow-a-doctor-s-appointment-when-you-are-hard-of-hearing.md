You can follow a doctor's appointment when you are hard of hearing by sorting out communication before you arrive, using one or two ways to read what is said during the visit, and getting the important parts in writing before you leave. Each step below takes a few minutes and covers three moments: the booking call, the consulting room and the time after you leave.

This is general information drawn from the sources linked below. It is not medical or legal advice. Your doctor, your audiologist or a disability rights organisation can tell you what applies to you.

## What should you do before a doctor's appointment?

Before a doctor's appointment, tell the practice you are hard of hearing and say exactly what you will need. The Hearing Loss Association of America (HLAA), a US consumer group, advises telling the receptionist that you are hard of hearing or deaf, saying what your communication needs are, and asking for the office manager's name in case you need help getting the services [2].

A short checklist for the booking:

1. Say that you are hard of hearing or deaf, and which aids you use: captioning, a sign language interpreter, a hearing loop or written notes.
2. Ask whether you can fill in forms online, so you are not called to a desk and asked questions you cannot lip-read [2].
3. Repeat back the date and time, and ask for an email confirmation [2].
4. Ask for a text number or email address to use if you will be late [2].
5. If you use a captioned phone or relay operator, tell the receptionist that the call may take longer [2].

HLAA also describes a Communication Access Plan (CAP): a written note of what helps you, which should become part of your medical record [2]. You can email or fax it ahead if the office allows [2]. Writing it once means you do not explain it at every visit.

## What can you say, word for word?

Short scripts make the first minute easier. These are our wording, built on what the Hearing Loss Association of America (HLAA) suggests [2]:

- On the phone: "I am hard of hearing. For the appointment I will need captioning or an interpreter. Can you note that on my file and send a confirmation by email?" [2]
- At reception: "I am hard of hearing. Here is my communication plan. Could someone come and find me when it is my turn, or use a vibrating pager?" [2]
- In the room: "I have hearing loss. Could you write down the plan and the medicines? I will repeat back what I understood." [2]
- At the end: "Who can I contact with questions, and by text or email?" [2]

## What are your rights at a doctor's appointment in the US and England?

In the US, the Americans with Disabilities Act (ADA) aims for communication with people who have disabilities to be "equally effective" as communication with people who do not [1]. The ADA.gov page on effective communication lists real-time captioning (also called CART) among the aids for people who are deaf or have hearing loss, and says it is particularly useful for those who do not use sign language [1]. It also says that in a doctor's office an interpreter will generally be needed for a patient who uses sign language [1].

Who chooses the aid depends on the provider. Under the ADA, state and local government entities must give primary consideration to the aid the person asks for, while businesses and nonprofits are encouraged to consult with the person [1]. HLAA's guide adds that the services you use for an outpatient visit are at no cost to you, your family or your caregiver [2]. Ask the practice to confirm this for your case.

In England, NHS and adult social care services have to follow the Accessible Information Standard [3]. One NHS trust describes six steps: services ask about your communication needs at the earliest opportunity, record them in your health record, flag them for staff, share them when appropriate, make sure you get information in the format you need, and review them over time [3]. The standard is published by NHS England, and this description comes from a trust that follows it, so check NHS England's own page for the full wording. Its example of communication support is a British Sign Language (BSL) interpreter, and it names d/Deaf people among those who may need accessible information [3].

## How do you follow the conversation in the consulting room?

In the consulting room, tell the doctor you have hearing loss, hand over your plan, and check understanding by repeating things back. HLAA suggests the patient says they have hearing loss, asks questions until all are answered, and repeats back information to make sure it was understood [2]. It also suggests asking that the details of your treatment plan and medicines come in writing, and asking for a contact name and number for questions about your care [2].

Three things from HLAA's list are easy to forget under stress:

- Ask whether a new medicine might make hearing, balance or tinnitus worse [2].
- If you have a cochlear implant, tell the doctor before an MRI [2].
- Ask staff to come and find you rather than call your name, or to use a vibrating pager [2].

For the conversation itself, a second channel helps. Captio AI, a live captioning app for deaf and hard of hearing people, turns speech into text as people talk [5]. At a [doctor's appointment](/use-cases/doctor-appointments), our suggestion is to ask the doctor if you may use it, then lay the phone on the desk between you. The captions label each speaker separately, so you know who said what [5].

## Which words and names should you prepare for a specialist appointment?

For a specialist appointment, add the terms you expect to hear before you go, and write down your questions in advance. Unfamiliar condition and drug names are easy to get wrong when you only hear them once, as our suggestion. Captio AI lets you add your own terms so conversations are captioned more accurately [5]. This list of terms does not need to be long: three or four from your referral letter is a good start, as our suggestion.

## How do you remember what was said after you leave?

After the appointment, rely on writing, not memory. HLAA recommends getting information about your treatment plan and medicines in writing [2]. If you used captions, you can save the recording as a transcript. ## How many people does this affect?

The World Health Organization (WHO) says over 5% of the world's population, or 430 million people, require rehabilitation for their disabling hearing loss, and that nearly 2.5 billion people are projected to have some degree of hearing loss by 2050 [4]. The same fact sheet says hard of hearing people usually communicate through spoken language and can benefit from hearing aids, cochlear implants, other assistive devices and captioning [4].

## Which option fits which appointment?

| Situation | What to try first | Why |
|---|---|---|
| GP or family doctor visit in England | Ask for your needs to be recorded under the Accessible Information Standard [3] | Your needs are flagged for staff involved in your care [3] |
| Appointment in a US doctor's office | Ask for real-time captioning or an interpreter when you book [1] | The ADA lists both as aids |
| Short pharmacy or reception conversation | Phone on the counter with live captions, plus a written note | Quick, no booking needed, as our suggestion |
| Long specialist consultation | Communication Access Plan, terms added in advance, treatment plan in writing [2] | Most information, highest stakes |

Captio AI is one tool among these. It does not replace a qualified interpreter or a captioner where one is your right, and it does not replace asking the doctor to repeat something. It is also not a phone-call or video-call captioning service. For a telephone appointment, ask the practice about relay or call-captioning services.
